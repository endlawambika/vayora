A

Need recognition: actual state vs desired state

A need starts as a gap between how a room is and how someone wants it to feel. The home page's first job is to make that gap visible.

Actual state

  • {{icon:cloud-fog}}Dust and pollution entering the room
  • {{icon:bug-off}}Mosquito disturbance
  • {{icon:wind}}Limited ventilation
  • {{icon:package}}Bulky devices that stay put
The gap is the needRecognised when it feels big enough to act on

Desired state

  • {{icon:filter}}Cleaner incoming air
  • {{icon:wind}}Better ventilation
  • {{icon:shield-check}}Mosquito protection
  • {{icon:feather}}A convenient, portable solution
Internal stimuliFelt discomfort: a stuffy room, an itchy bite, a dusty sill noticed at the end of the day.
External stimuliCues from outside: our problem cards, the hazy-window hero image, a roommate's recommendation.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Need recognition happens when a consumer notices a meaningful difference between their actual state and a desired state. Internal stimuli (what they feel) and external stimuli (what they see or hear) can both trigger it.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Many people treat dust, mosquitoes and stuffiness as normal. The problem is real but sits in the background, an existing need that is only sometimes active.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • "Your room should feel like your safe space. Does it?"
  • The three problem cards, shown as everyday moments
  • The hero image: hazy air outside, clean air inside
  • "What bothers you most?" tiles let visitors name their own gap

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

We open with a question rather than a statistic, so visitors check their own experience. Recognising the gap themselves is more persuasive, and more honest, than being told they have a problem.

B

Active vs inactive problems

Some visitors already know their room has an air problem. Others have never thought about whether their purifier brings in fresh air at all.

Active: already awareInactive: not yet noticed

For active-problem visitors

They're already comparing options. We give them depth: the exploded view, the spec table and an honest comparison.

For inactive-problem visitors

We show the everyday moment (open window, dust and mosquitoes) and explain the difference between recirculating and fresh air.

{{icon:triangle-alert}}What we avoid: fear appeals. No alarming pollution statistics, disease claims or images of illness. The problem is framed as everyday discomfort, not danger.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

An active problem is one the consumer already recognises. An inactive problem exists, but the consumer isn't aware of it yet, so communication has to create that awareness first.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Dust and mosquitoes are mostly active problems. The lack of fresh-air ventilation, because most purifiers only recirculate, is often inactive.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • "Fresh air, filtered first" on the home page
  • The recirculation vs fresh-air illustration
  • The first row of the comparison table

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Explaining a difference people hadn't considered activates the problem through understanding, not anxiety. Fear might create urgency, but it would cost trust that a brand with no track record can't afford to lose.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Motivation is the drive to reduce the tension a need creates. Maslow orders needs from physiological to self-actualisation; Self-Determination Theory says people are most motivated when they feel autonomy, competence and relatedness.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Vayora touches several levels at once, which helps. But as a new ₹4,999 device, it also creates an approach–avoidance conflict: the benefits pull people in, the risks hold them back.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • Benefits for comfort and safety
  • Freedom, comfort, control for autonomy
  • The price questions, which answer the worries head-on, for the conflict

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Rather than pushing harder on the "want", we lower the "worry". Transparency and an accessible target price reduce the avoidance side of the conflict.

D

Consumer perception

Exposure gets Vayora seen. Attention makes it noticed. Interpretation makes sure it's understood correctly.

{{icon:search}}ExposureA headline that states the promise in five words, a hero image of the product in a window, and page titles written for search and sharing.
{{icon:eye}}AttentionOne focal image per section, forest green on off-white for contrast, and motion used only for the airflow line.
{{icon:brain}}InterpretationLabelled components, plain-language stages, and badges that say exactly how certain each claim is.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Perception is how people select, organise and interpret what they encounter. Selective attention filters out most stimuli, and too much information at once leads people to give up.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Vayora looks like a familiar category but works differently, so misinterpretation is a real risk. "Is it an AC?" is the question we most need to prevent.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • The hero: one promise, one image, two actions
  • The exploded view reveals one component at a time
  • The "not an air conditioner" callout, placed before any feature list
  • The hero carousel always opens on the core promise, gives each later slide one benefit, and pauses on hover, on focus and for reduced motion
  • Highlight tabs show one topic at a time

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Progressive disclosure (select a stage, open a question) keeps each screen light. Attention isn't spread thin, and the product is interpreted correctly the first time.

E

Consumer learning and memory

A new brand gets remembered through a few strong, repeated associations, not many weak ones.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Consumer learning is how repeated exposure and experience build associations in memory. Habits form through a loop: a cue triggers a routine, which delivers a reward.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Vayora needs to be recognisable at a glance. In use, opening a window or returning to the room could become the cue to switch it on, and a more comfortable room the intended reward.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • The same palette and pod silhouette in every illustration
  • The airflow line in the hero, the stepper and the exploded view
  • The tagline and pod-shaped logo in every footer
  • The announcement strip repeats three core messages on every page
  • Familiar Indian electronics-retail patterns: offer strip, mega menu, category tiles, big footer

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Consistent cues reduce the effort of recognition. Borrowing the layout conventions shoppers already know from Indian appliance brands means visitors spend no effort learning the site, only learning the product. We present the habit loop as a proposal, not an established behaviour, because nobody has used Vayora yet.

F

Consumer attitudes: the ABC model

Understanding, feeling and action each have their own parts of the site.

C

Cognitive

"I understand how the device works."

Follow-the-air stepper, exploded view, spec table, FAQ

A

Affective

"I feel reassured, comfortable and interested."

Hero illustration, lifestyle scenes, the "feel" statement, honest labels

B

Behavioural

"I'll join the waitlist."

Waitlist button on every page, a five-field form

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

An attitude has three components: cognitive (beliefs and knowledge), affective (feelings) and behavioural (intentions and actions).

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

For an unfamiliar electronic product, good feelings without understanding won't last, and understanding without reassurance won't lead to action.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • Cognitive: Follow the air, spec table
  • Affective: "Because feeling comfortable at home…"
  • Behavioural: final call to action

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

For a considered purchase, pages follow a learn, then feel, then do order: explain first, reassure next, then invite a small, low-commitment action.

Financial risk

₹4,999 is a real amount on a student or first-job budget.

Functional risk

Will it fit my window? Will it be easy to use?

Performance risk

Does the filtration actually work? Nobody has tested it yet.

Risk bars are our qualitative judgement of relative concern, not survey data.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Involvement is how personally relevant and risky a purchase feels. Higher involvement leads to more information search and more careful evaluation before buying.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Perceived financial, functional and performance risk is high for a new device with no reviews, so most visitors will need real information before they act.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • Product mechanism and feature explanations
  • Pricing transparency and comparisons
  • FAQs and the waitlist
  • The lab timeline sets honest expectations for what happens next

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Each element answers the question a visitor has at one stage of the journey. The waitlist is a low-risk next step that matches the uncertainty of a pre-launch product, instead of forcing a purchase decision.

H

Information search and the evoked set

People trust some sources more than others. Right now, Vayora only has the least trusted one: its own website.

{{icon:users}}Personal

Friends, family and roommates. Clear, repeatable positioning makes Vayora easy to describe to someone else.

Encouraged, never scripted
{{icon:message-circle}}Commercial

This website and any future advertising. Useful for information, but naturally viewed with some scepticism.

Live now
{{icon:scale}}Independent

Reviews and comparison platforms. These don't exist for Vayora yet, and we won't invent them.

None yet
{{icon:hand}}Experiential

Product demonstrations and prototype testing. The most convincing source, planned with waitlist members.

Planned
{{art:sets}}

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Consumers search memory and four kinds of external source: personal, commercial, independent and experiential. The result is an evoked set, the few options they seriously consider.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Fans, mosquito nets and purifiers are already in most people's evoked sets. Vayora has to earn a place among them by being clearly positioned against what people already know.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • The category comparison on Pricing
  • The audience cards, with quotes clearly marked as illustrative
  • Site search (press /) covers features, FAQs and concepts
  • The waitlist's optional "volunteer for prototype testing" box recruits the experiential source
  • No testimonials anywhere, on purpose

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Real testimonials and product reviews should only be added after genuine customers have used the product. Inventing them would fake the independent source people rely on most, and break trust the moment it was discovered.

I

Attitude formation and persuasion: ELM

Some visitors read every spec. Others decide in a glance. The site is built for both.

{{art:elm}}

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

The Elaboration Likelihood Model says persuasion takes a central route (careful thought about arguments) when motivation and ability are high, or a peripheral route (cues like design and attractiveness) when they're low. Central-route attitudes tend to last longer.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Someone evaluating a ₹4,999 electronic product wants meaningful information on filtration, battery life, design, portability and price. A casual visitor responds to visual appeal, brand identity and simple messaging.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • Central: mechanism, spec table, price rationale
  • Peripheral: the hero, product render, palette and tagline

{{icon:compass}}Why both routes matter

Peripheral cues earn the click; central-route content earns the decision. Social proof, normally a strong peripheral cue, is deliberately absent until it can be real.

J

Source credibility and two-sided messaging

A student startup has no track record. Trust has to come from candour.

{{icon:circle-check}}What we say

  • Transparent specifications, every one labelled
  • Preliminary targets marked as targets
  • Honest limitations: needs a window gap, isn't an AC, hasn't been tested
  • A promise to share prototype testing updates
  • Authentic customer feedback, once it exists

{{icon:circle-x}}What we don't say

  • Customer testimonials or sales figures
  • Certifications or endorsements
  • HEPA efficiency or PM2.5 removal percentages
  • Verified battery performance
  • That it cools a room or prevents disease
The labels used across the site:Target specProposedTo be validated

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Source credibility combines perceived expertise and trustworthiness. Two-sided messages, which admit weaknesses alongside strengths, tend to be more credible, especially with informed audiences.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Vayora can't borrow credibility from reviews, certifications or a known brand. The only credibility available is being visibly honest about what's known and what isn't.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • "Works with windows fully closed: No" in the comparison
  • The status column in the spec table
  • Ongoing costs marked "To be determined" in the price FAQ

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Admitting limits up front protects against disappointment later, and makes the positive claims easier to believe.

K

External influences: culture, reference groups and family

Where and how people live in urban India shapes what they need, and who has a say in buying it.

{{icon:school}}Hostel and PG living

Shared rooms, fixed fittings and rules about what can be installed.

{{icon:building-2}}Rented apartments

Landlord permission, security deposits and moving every year or two.

{{icon:house}}Family decision-making

Parents often fund or approve a student's purchase; families decide together on home devices.

{{icon:users}}Friends and roommates

A device in a shared room affects everyone in it, so roommates influence the choice.

{{icon:message-circle}}Word of mouth

Recommendations from people like us carry more weight than any advert.

{{icon:wallet}}Urban lifestyle and affordability

Busy routines, power cuts and tight budgets make convenience and price matter.

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Culture, reference groups and family shape needs, preferences and who makes the final decision. Reference groups also provide the standards people compare themselves against.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

A student might want Vayora, a parent might pay for it, and a roommate might live with it. The message has to make sense to all three.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • The three audience scenes with relatable situations
  • "Who we're designing it for"
  • Pricing language: "a price a student or tenant can plan for"

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Recognisable situations make the message feel written for the visitor, and give them simple words to pass on to a parent or roommate.

L

Ethical consumer behaviour and choice architecture

Make the right next step easy, and never trick anyone into it.

{{icon:circle-check}}Friction we removed

  • {{icon:check}}Clear navigation: four pages, the same on every page
  • {{icon:check}}Transparent pricing: one number, with its caveat beside it
  • {{icon:check}}Simple signup: five fields, no account, no payment
  • {{icon:check}}Clear product explanations, in plain language
  • {{icon:check}}Visible FAQs, with "not yet known" when that's the answer
  • {{icon:check}}Honest communication about limits
  • {{icon:check}}Search, help and FAQs reachable from every page
  • {{icon:check}}Prototype-testing opt-in left unticked by default

{{icon:circle-x}}Patterns we refused

  • {{icon:x}}Countdown timers and manufactured urgency
  • {{icon:x}}"Only 3 left" or other fabricated scarcity
  • {{icon:x}}Fake testimonials or star ratings
  • {{icon:x}}Misleading health claims
  • {{icon:x}}Pre-ticked consent boxes
  • {{icon:x}}Guilt-tripping "No thanks" buttons

{{icon:book-open}}What it means

Choice architecture is the way options are presented, and it always influences decisions. Ethical design makes the beneficial choice easy without exploiting cognitive biases.

{{icon:lightbulb}}How it applies

Air quality feels like a health topic, which makes manipulation especially easy and especially harmful. A waitlist with no payment keeps the stakes low for the visitor.

{{icon:eye}}Where you see it

  • The waitlist button in the header of every page
  • The form's prototype notice: data stays in your browser
  • The student-project statement in every footer
  • The announcement strip carries status updates, never fake offers or countdowns
  • The floating Help button offers search and answers, not sales pressure

{{icon:compass}}Why we designed it this way

Dark patterns might lift signups in the short term, but they would turn a trust-based brand into one nobody believes. Reducing friction is the persuasion.
